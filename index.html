Check 1 · Course policyDecision guide

Because this involves your own experience or reflection, keep the ideas and final wording yours. Even editing help must fit your instructor’s policy.

Should I use AI for this?

Use these checks in order. If an answer is unclear, pause and ask your instructor.

  1. Check your course policy. If AI is prohibited, do the work without it. If the rules are unclear, ask first.
  2. Check assessment rules. For exams or quizzes, use AI only with explicit permission for the specific use.
  3. Keep reflection personal. Your experiences, ideas, and final wording should remain yours.
  4. Verify the output. If you cannot evaluate its claims, read course materials and real sources first.
  5. Protect private information. Leave out sensitive data and material you do not have permission to share.
  6. Be transparent. Follow disclosure requirements and describe the help accurately.
Plan a responsible approach

Same tool.
Different boundaries.

What makes sense depends on the task. Each example assumes your course allows the specific kind of help described.

Personal essay
Keep it personal.

Possible support: Feedback on grammar or clarity in a draft you wrote, if permitted.

Keep yours: Your experiences, perspective, and final wording. Do not ask AI to invent a personal story or write the reflection for you.

Group presentation
Build on shared thinking.

Possible support: Brainstorm an outline or discussion questions. Compare any summary against the original reading.

Keep yours: Your group’s argument, selected sources, and interpretation. Agree on AI use and disclosure as a group.

Research paper
Go back to the source.

Possible support: Refine a question, identify search terms, or explore ways to organize your argument.

Keep yours: Research, evidence, and analysis. Locate and read real sources; never treat a chatbot’s citation as proof that a source exists.

Exam or quiz
Explicit permission first.

The boundary: Only use AI when assessment instructions explicitly allow it and explain how.

That includes checking answers. A take-home format does not automatically make AI use acceptable. When in doubt, ask before using it.

Use it to support
the work you do.

Permission is the starting point. When AI is allowed, choose a limited role that helps you understand and improve your own work.

Brainstorm possibilities

Ask for questions or angles to consider. Choose what fits the assignment, discard what does not, and develop the ideas yourself.

Shape an outline

Use a suggested structure as something to evaluate. Revise it around your argument and fill it with evidence you have read.

Clarify a reading

Ask for an explanation of a difficult concept. Return to the original text to check what was simplified, omitted, or misrepresented.

Review your draft

Request feedback on clarity, organization, or grammar against the assignment rubric. Review each suggestion and make the final decisions.

A useful boundary

Avoid outsourcing a full essay, inventing citations, or using generated language to simulate understanding you have not developed.

Sound like yourself.
Think for yourself.

Polished wording is not the same as a strong argument. Your work should reflect what you understand and can explain.

  • Start with your own claim, notes, or draft.
  • Use course readings and examples you chose.
  • Remove vague claims and language you would not use.
  • Be able to explain every argument and source.

Make the help visible.

Describe your actual process and follow your instructor’s required format. Adapt this example only if it is accurate:

“I used an AI tool to suggest an outline and flag unclear sentences. I revised the structure, checked the sources, and made the final writing decisions.”

Keep prompts or drafts if your course asks for them. A disclosure does not make prohibited use acceptable.

Before you submit,
take a second look.

If you cannot answer these questions confidently, pause and change your approach.

  • Can I verify it?Check factual claims, quotations, and citations against credible sources or course materials.
  • Can I explain my process?Identify where AI helped and describe that help honestly.
  • Did I protect private information?Do not share personal, sensitive, or confidential material, or someone else’s work without permission.
  • Does it meet the assignment’s rules?Follow the specific policy, including restrictions and disclosure requirements.

The thinking
behind the guide.

The original college project connected practical choices to course discussions of AI, power, and responsibility. These are the project’s takeaways, not direct quotations.

Crawford
Treat AI as infrastructure that depends on data and labor, rather than as magic. Consider what you put into a system.
Noble & Benjamin
Question how bias can be reflected in systems and reproduced in writing. Read outputs critically rather than accepting them at face value.
Gunkel
Question the impulse to treat systems as neutral or humanlike. Use explicit checks instead of relying on how convincing an answer sounds.

Based on the course connections in Jacob’s original student guide. The supplied project did not include specific reading titles or page references; no bibliography has been invented for this iteration.

A guide everyone
can work through.

Accessibility is part of the experience, from the reading order to the next question. These practices replace the original checklist.

  • Keyboard access: Use Tab and Shift+Tab to move; Enter or Space to activate buttons. Focus stays visible.
  • Clear structure: Semantic headings, landmarks, and a skip link support navigation.
  • Understandable steps: Each question has labeled choices. The tool moves focus to the new question or result.
  • Readable content: High-contrast text, comfortable line lengths, and layouts that reflow when you zoom.
  • Reduced motion: Your device’s reduced-motion setting disables scroll and transition animation.
  • Useful alternatives: The decision checks remain readable without JavaScript.

Image strategy: this version uses text rather than content images. Decorative marks are hidden from assistive technology. Any future informative image needs alt text that describes its purpose; decorative images should use empty alt text.
